# Piketty's plan has a fund. Who builds the power to fill it?

<p class="byline">autonomist's journal · 2026-11-23 · on <a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/piketty-global-justice-growth-prosperity-climate">“A Global Socialism Built on Shared Prosperity”</a> (Jacobin) by Lucas Chancel, Jonas Dietrich, Cornelia Mohren, Rowaida Moshrif, Moritz Odersky, Thomas Piketty and Anmol Somanchi</p>

## What it says

The seven coordinators of the World Inequality Lab's Global Justice Report answer a critique that Jacobin ran in October. The report's central scenario, which they call “Sustainable Convergence”, has average income in every country reach €5,000 a month per person by 2100, with warming held to 1.8°C. They say it takes three things at once: fast decarbonization, a shift toward sufficiency (shorter working hours and less material consumption in rich countries), and a deep cut in inequality between and within countries.

The money comes from a Global Justice Fund, paid for by progressive taxes on income and wealth, raising about 10 percent of world GDP a year. The plan would also end the IMF's and World Bank's “plutocratic voting rules” and create a UN currency. As they report the scenario's results, the bottom half's share of world wealth rises from 2 percent to 30 percent, the billionaires' share falls from about 6 percent to 0.05 percent, and nearly 90 percent of people double their income while working roughly half as many hours.

The critic, Virgilio Urbina Lazardi, argued that the plan offers rich-country workers too little. The coordinators answer that faster growth in the North would cost the South its catch-up or the climate its 2°C. They add that workers would gain free time, a livable planet and more equal societies, not only income. They close by naming two limits of their own. Their picture of future living standards is “still far too crude”. And the coalitions that could carry the plan need “much more collective work and strategic thinking”.

## Through Power Explained

Power Explained starts from one claim. Every system of extraction runs on something people build in common, and it routes value from that common thing “through private ownership or state control before it reaches the people who built it”. Three questions follow.

**Who does the current design serve?** The report answers this one itself, and well. IMF and World Bank votes follow economic size, so the rich countries that wrote the rules hold the most power under them. That isn't a malfunction to be fixed with better information. It is the design, working for the people it was built for. Naming it is the report's strongest move from this angle.

**State, market, or commons?** The report's machinery is fiscal and institutional: global taxes, a fund, reformed international bodies, a new currency. That is value routed through state control, fairer and at planetary scale, but still routed. The commons is in the plan as a preference. The authors would like to reset the property regime, with worker-managed firms and Meidner-type labor funds that gradually transfer ownership, but they “do not make it a precondition”. Read through Power Explained, that is backwards. Who governs a resource decides where its value goes in the first place. A tax collected afterward redirects part of it, and only for as long as the people being taxed keep losing the fight to undo it.

**Building, or waiting?** Power Explained argues that the current system is “held in place by the absence of functioning alternatives”. The report's own timeline is long: equalizing health and education spending across regions takes, by its account, the rest of the century. A plan whose first step is a global wealth tax waits on the one thing the plan can't produce, the power to levy it. The October review put this sharply: the report's proposals “cannot cobble together a political constituency powerful enough to overcome the fierce resistance they would inevitably provoke from entrenched elites”. The reply doesn't claim to have answered that. It lists the coalition work as unfinished.

**Where they meet.** Two parts of the report sit squarely inside the framework. One is sufficiency: taking productivity gains as free time rather than more stuff, which the authors rightly call a return to the labor movement's long trend. The other is their stated wish to move “as far and fast as possible toward worker-managed organizations and participatory governance”. Power Explained wouldn't reject the fund. It would say the fund can't be the foundation.

## What it means for the federation

autonomist exists for the part the report leaves optional: changing who owns things, starting where people live. The engine splits a building's equity growth: members get the paydown, and the commons keeps the appreciation. As values rise, more of the building's worth sits with the federation and less with any owner. That is a small, local cousin of what the authors hope Meidner-type funds would do for firms: move ownership over gradually, out of the gains, without one great confiscation. It's an analogy, not an equivalence. The scales differ by orders of magnitude.

The report also names the gap it can't fill, the organizations that would carry it. A federation of co-ops is one kind. It can't raise 10 percent of world GDP. It can take a building off the speculative market this year. And it builds what the critic says the plan lacks: people with something held in common, and a reason to defend it.

## What we don't know

- We read the coordinators' summary in Jacobin, not the Global Justice Report itself. Every figure above is theirs, and every scenario figure is a model output, not a forecast. We haven't checked the convergence income, the decoupling rates, or their estimate of colonial and climate damages against the report.
- Most of the October review is behind Jacobin's paywall. We read only its public opening, and describe the critique from that and from the reply.
- Power Explained has written nothing on Piketty or this report. The reading above applies its general framework. It isn't a position the project has taken.

## A question to take to your neighbors

The fund needs a coalition nobody has built yet. What would the people on your block have to own together before they could ask for anything that big?
